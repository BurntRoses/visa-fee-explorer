About the data in this report

Permanent Resident Admissions dataFootnote 1 is presented in Tables 1 through 4, as well as on the Government of Canada’s Open Government Portal.

In accordance with the Privacy Act, IRCC has implemented data rounding practices to help protect an individual’s privacy when publishing statistical data. Data are suppressed or rounded to prevent the identification of individuals when the figures are compiled and compared with other publicly available statistics. Values between 1 and 5 are shown as “–” and all other values are rounded to the nearest multiple of 5. The value zero is reported. This may result in the sum of the figures not equating to the totals indicated. Please note that this applies to all data from 2024 onwards.

In this report, data related to the Economic and Humanitarian and Compassionate and Other Permanent Resident categories differs from that found in the Open Data Portal. Work is underway to update the Open Data Portal to align these figures across sources going forward.

In addition to the Annual Report, Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) also produces the Departmental Results Report (DRR). The DRR provides parliamentarians and Canadians with information on the Department’s performance against the expected results outlined in the Departmental Results Framework and Departmental Plan. It follows the structure of IRCC’s Core Responsibilities, as approved by the Treasury Board Secretariat. The DRR includes broader data and insights than those found in the Annual Report.

Please note that the DRR and the Annual Report may be tabled at separate times. If the Annual Report is published first, there may be a period during which the most recent DRR is not yet available.

Processing times for immigration applications are published and updated monthly on IRCC’s website. Readers are directed to the following link for the most up-to-date information: Check our current processing times.

Message from the minister

As Minister of Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship, I am pleased to table the 2026 Annual Report to Parliament on Immigration.

Well-managed immigration remains central to Canada’s economic growth and the vitality of communities across the country. In 2025, we enhanced our global competitiveness with new initiatives to attract and retain people with the skills and experience the country needs. This more targeted approach advances Canada’s immediate and longer-term goals and gives newcomers a stronger foundation for success.

Last year, Canada welcomed 393,770 permanent residents, in line with the 2025-2027 Immigration Levels Plan. Nearly six in ten newcomers arrived through economic immigration programs, helping to address labour shortages and drive innovation and growth. We prioritized the workers we need through Express Entry, with almost 115,000 people selected for their French-language proficiency or experience in high-demand fields such as health care, social services, skilled trades, and education. Canada exceeded its target for welcoming French-speaking permanent residents outside Quebec for the fourth year in a row, supporting demographic renewal and helping Francophone minority communities flourish.

The Provincial Nominee Program, Atlantic Immigration Program and community immigration pilots accounted for just over half of economic admissions outside Quebec. These pathways bolster regional economies while connecting newcomers with opportunities outside major urban centres. Ongoing collaboration with provincial, territorial, and municipal partners keeps our immigration system attuned to their needs and capacity.

Planning temporary and permanent immigration together is bringing greater balance to the system. As part of this coordinated approach, we transitioned nearly 170,000 temporary workers and students with the skills and experience Canada needs to permanent residence in 2025.

We remain committed to reuniting families and upholding Canada’s humanitarian tradition. In 2025, nearly 100,000 parents, grandparents, children, spouses, and other family members were reunited with loved ones in Canada, while almost 60,000 refugees and protected persons found a permanent home here. The government also advanced legislation to modernize the in-Canada asylum system, strengthen its integrity and support more timely decisions while preserving protection for those who need it.

The success of immigration depends as much on the people Canada welcomes as the way we welcome them. In 2025, over half a million people accessed settlement services, including pre-arrival supports, language training and employment preparation. These services help newcomers make full use of their skills and establish themselves in their communities.

By managing immigration with care, purpose, and integrity, we can rebuild public confidence, help newcomers and communities thrive and shape a stronger Canada for generations to come.

Sincerely,

The Hon. Lena Metlege Diab, ECNS, KC, PC, MP
Minister of Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship

Introduction

The Annual Report to Parliament on Immigration provides Parliamentarians and Canadians with valuable information on permanent and temporary immigration. The Immigration and Refugee Protection Act (IRPA) requires that this report be tabled in Parliament each year. The report includes data for 2025 by immigration category. It shows whether admissions and arrivals were within the targets and ranges set out in the Government of Canada’s 2025-2027 Immigration Levels Plan.

A return to sustainable immigration levels

The Government is focused on returning immigration levels to a more sustainable pace by reducing temporary resident arrivals and maintaining stable permanent resident admissions. The 2025–2027 Immigration Levels Plan continued to prioritize economic immigration, while decreasing overall admissions targets for permanent residents. For the first time, it also included targets for temporary residents, constituting a new holistic approach to managed migration. Targets were set for new arrivals of international students and temporary foreign workers with the primary aim of returning volumes to more sustainable levels, while also taking into account the needs of our labour market and Canadian employers. To support the commitment to reduce the share of non-permanent residents to less than 5% of the population, the number of new arrivals were also balanced with planned departures of international students and temporary workers.

Temporary resident programs and volumes

A temporary resident is a foreign national who is authorized to enter Canada for a limited period. Temporary resident status may be granted to an individual who meets the legislative requirements to enter or remain in Canada as a visitor, student, worker, or holder of a temporary resident permit.

Total permit holders

Throughout 2025, 3,341,695 people held valid temporary resident permits to enter or remain in Canada. This total includes individuals who received a new permit or an extension during the year, as well as those who remained in Canada with valid status throughout the year.

The figure includes temporary residents holding permits as visitors, students, workers, or temporary resident permit holders. It is adjusted to account for individuals that may hold more than one type of permit in a year.

Temporary resident arrivals

The 2025-2027 Immigration Levels Plan is the first to include targets for arrivals of Temporary Residents. Temporary Resident arrivals are a subset of the total of Work and Study permits signed. The number of arrivals in the levels plan does not include:

  • asylum claimants
  • study/work permit applicants in Canada (permit extensions)
  • seasonal agricultural workers and certain other seasonal workers under the Temporary Foreign Worker Program (TFWP) who are in Canada for 270 days or less and whose work period begins and ends within the same calendar year.

In 2025, there were 323,995 temporary resident arrivals, which was below the target range of 604,900 to 742,400 set out in the 2025-2027 Levels Plan. Of these arrivals, 153,920 entered through the International Mobility Program (IMP), 54,895 through the TFWP, and 115,180 as study permit holders.

International students

IRCC facilitates the entry of students who want to study at a designated educational institution in Canada. Individuals approved to study in Canada are issued a study permit.

In 2025, 381,725 individuals had their study permit become effective (including extensions).Footnote 6 Of those whose new permit or extension became effective, 184,245 were female, 197,410 were male, and a small portion were another gender or unspecified. The number of study permits that became effective decreased by 26.1% compared with 2024. In 2024, 516,275 people had a study permit become effective.

In 2025, females accounted for 48.3% of individuals holding study permits that became effective, up from 47.1% in 2024. The total number of study permits that became effective fell significantly in 2025 compared with 2024. The decrease was larger among males than among females.

India remained the largest source country for study permits in 2025, accounting for 24.6% of all permits that became effective. China was the second-largest source country, followed by Nigeria and the Philippines.

The top five source countries came from several regions of the world, highlighting the broad international appeal of Canada’s education system and the continued interest in Canadian post-secondary credentials.

Student to permanent resident transition

In 2025, 20,790Footnote 9 former study permit holders became permanent residents. Of those, 10,435 were female and 10,355 were male. This represented a decrease of 18.7% compared with 2024, when 25,580 former study permit holders became permanent residents.

Temporary workers

Foreign nationals seeking to temporarily work in Canada may be authorized to work without a permit or receive a work permit under one of Canada’s work permit programs under the Immigration and Refugee Protection Regulations.

IRCC manages the International Mobility Program (IMP) and jointly administers the Temporary Foreign Worker Program (TFWP) with Employment and Social Development Canada. These programs help employers address labour needs while supporting Canada’s economic and social objectives. Under the TFWP, employers must obtain a Labour Market Impact Assessment (LMIA) to show that no qualified Canadian citizen or permanent resident is available for the job. Under the IMP, employers can hire certain foreign workers without an LMIA when there are broader economic, social, cultural, or reciprocal benefits for Canada.

In 2025, 743,770Footnote 10 people had a work permit become effective for employment purposes.Footnote 11 This total includes permits issued through both the TFWP and the IMP. Of these, 256,880 were new work permits and 491,840 were permit extensions. The number of people with a work permit that became effective decreased by 17.9% compared with 2024. In 2024, 905,440 people had a work permit become effective.

In 2025, males accounted for 58.3% of work permits that became effective, while females accounted for 41.7%. This continues the pattern of males representing the larger share of work permit holders. The number of work permits that became effective declined for both females and males between 2024 and 2025. The decrease was slightly greater among females. This gender distribution may reflect the sectors that make the greatest use of temporary foreign workers. Industries such as agriculture, construction, transportation, and skilled trades often employ a higher proportion of males, which may contribute to the larger share of male work permit holders.

In 2025, 166,790 individuals had TFWP permits become effective. Of those, 33,210 were female, 133,575 as male, and a small portion were another gender or did not specify a gender.

During the same year, 580,125 people had a work permit become effective through the IMP. Of these, 277,810 were female, 302,265 were male, and a small number were another gender or did not specify a gender.

Females represented a larger share of work permit holders under the IMP (47.9%) than under the TFWP (19.9%). This difference reflects the types of jobs and sectors served by each program. IRCC continues to monitor gender outcomes across work permit programs, including the Open Work Permit for Vulnerable Workers. In 2025, females accounted for 27.8% of approvals under this permit.

India was the largest source country for people whose work permits became effective in 2025, accounting for 28.9% of the total. Ukraine was the second-largest source country, followed by Mexico. The continued presence of Ukrainian nationals among the top source countries reflects Canada’s temporary measures to support people affected by Russia’s invasion of Ukraine.

The top five source countries came from different regions of the world, highlighting the diverse origins of temporary foreign workers and other foreign nationals working in Canada through temporary resident programs.

Open Work Permit for Vulnerable workers

Every worker in Canada is entitled to a safe and healthy work environment. Temporary foreign workers who are experiencing abuse, or who are at risk of abuse, may be eligible for an Open Work Permit for Vulnerable Workers (OWP-V). The OWP-V allows eligible workers with a valid employer-specific work permit to obtain an open work permit. This gives them the flexibility to leave an unsafe or abusive job and work for almost any employer in Canada while they look for a new position and apply for a new work permit.

In 2025, 10,190 people had an OWP-V become effective. Of those, 2,830 were female and 7,360 were male.

Temporary to permanent resident transition

In 2025, 145,735 temporary work permit holders became permanent residents. Of these, 70,120 were female, 75,600 were male, and 10 were another gender. This represented a decrease of 9% compared with 2024. In 2024, 160,100 temporary work permit holders became permanent residents.

Visitors

IRCC facilitates the entry of visitors who wish to stay in Canada temporarily, while safeguarding the health, safety, and security of Canadians.Footnote 14 Depending on their nationality, travel document, and method of travel, visitors may need a Temporary Resident Visa (TRV) or an Electronic Travel Authorization (eTA) to travel to Canada. IRCC processes TRVs and eTAs for visitors such as tourists, those seeking to visit family, business travellers, and those transiting through Canada. Visitors contribute to Canada’s economy by supporting businesses, creating demand for goods and services, and generating tourism revenue. Business visitors can also help strengthen trade and investment ties. In addition, visitors help maintain family, cultural, and social connections that contribute to Canada’s diverse and inclusive society.

In 2025, 4,833,235 TRVs and eTAs were issued. Of those, 2,399,540 were for individuals who were female, 2,433,530 were male, 80 were another gender, and 90 had their gender recorded as unspecified or unknown. The number of issued TRVs and eTAs decreased by 3.9% compared with 2024. In 2024, IRCC issued 5,031,925 TRVs and eTAs.

Temporary Resident Permits

Under subsection 24(1) of the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act (IRPA), a Temporary Resident Permit (TRP) may be issued to a foreign national who is inadmissible to Canada or otherwise does not meet the requirements of the Act. A TRP may be issued when an officer determines that the person’s need to enter or remain in Canada temporarily is justified in the circumstances. TRPs are issued for a limited period and may be cancelled at any time.

In 2025, 5,100 TRP applications were approved. More information about Temporary Resident Permits and inadmissibility is available on IRCC’s website.

TRPs for victims of human trafficking

To help protect victims of human trafficking, TRPs may be issued to foreign nationals in Canada who do not have legal immigration status. These permits give temporary resident status and allow holders to apply for a work permit.

In 2025, 80 TRP applications for victims of human trafficking and their dependants were approved. This number is included in the total number of TRPs shown in Table 2.

TRPs for victims of family violence

Since 2019, out-of-status foreign nationals who are experiencing family violence from a spouse or common-law partner can apply for a fee-exempt TRP. This measure is intended for individuals whose application for permanent residence depends on remaining in a genuine relationship that has become abusive. The TRP grants temporary legal status in Canada, enabling individuals to apply for an open work permit or study permit, and to safely leave an abusive relationship without risking their immigration status.

In 2025, 310 TRP applications were approved for victims of family violence and their dependants. These applications are processed separately from TRPs and are not included in the totals shown in Table 2.

Public policy exemptions for a temporary purpose

In 2025, 120 applications for temporary residence were approved under a public policy that provides exemptions from certain inadmissibility requirements. This policy allows eligible foreign nationals to come to Canada as visitors, students, or workers even if they would not normally meet all immigration requirements. These cases are processed separately from TRPs and are not included in the totals shown in Table 2.

Use of the negative discretion authority

Under subsection 22.1(4) of the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act (IRPA), the Annual Report must include the number of declarations made by the Minister stating that a foreign national (other than a person referred to in section 19) cannot become a temporary resident. This authority, known as negative discretion, may be used when the Minister determines that, because of public policy concerns, a foreign national is not eligible to become a temporary resident for a specific period, up to three years. The report must also describe the public policy reasons for any such declarations.

In 2025, the Minister of Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship did not use the negative discretion authority under subsection 22.1(1) of IRPA.

Permanent immigration

Foreign nationals can become permanent residents of Canada through a range of immigration pathways and programs. Permanent resident status allows people to live, work, and study anywhere in Canada. Those who meet the eligibility requirements may later apply for Canadian citizenship.

IRCC facilitates the entry of permanent residents in a way that maximizes their economic, social, and cultural contributions to Canada, while also safeguarding the health, safety, and security of all Canadians.

In 2025, IRCC admitted 393,770 permanent residents. This was within the target range of 367,000 to 436,000 set out in the 2025–2027 Immigration Levels Plan. It was slightly below the target of 395,000 admissions. Of those admitted, 203,320 were female, 190,425 were male, and 30 were another gender. In 2024, the department admitted 483,640 permanent residents. As a result, the number of permanent resident admissions in 2025 was 18.6% lower than in 2024.

Economic immigration

Canada offers a range of economic immigration programs to attract talent, address labour shortages, and support economic growth. These programs ensure that the benefits of immigration are shared across Canada. These programs select skilled workers and entrepreneurs who can contribute to Canada’s economy. The economic category was the largest source of permanent resident admissions in 2025; admitting 225,680 people and accounting for 57.3% of all admissions. This total includes principal applicants as well as their spouses and dependants. Of those admitted, 108,790 were female, 116,880 were male, and a small number were another gender. In 2024, 281,615 people were admitted through the economic programs. Admissions under economic immigration programs were 19.9% lower in 2025 than in 2024.

In 2025, females accounted for 48.2% of economic immigration admissions, a slight increase from 47.6% in 2024, though overall admissions for all applicants dropped. Between 2021 and 2025, the gender distribution of economic immigrants remained fairly stable. Over this period, the share of females increased slightly, while males continued to make up a small majority of admissions.

In 2025, India remained the largest source country for economic immigration, accounting for 30.2% of all admissions in this category. Compared with previous years, a larger share of admissions came from African countries, including Cameroon, Algeria, and Morocco. This change reflects, in part, the impact of category-based selection rounds that focused on French-language skills and work experience in targeted occupations.

Express Entry

Express Entry (EE) is Canada’s application management system for the Federal Skilled Worker Program, Federal Skilled Trades Program, Canadian Experience Class, and a portion of the Provincial Nominee Program. The EE system manages the intake of applications for permanent residence under key economic immigration programs, while facilitating the selection of individuals most likely to succeed. Category-Based Selection (CBS) enables the Minister to establish categories that support specific economic priorities and invite the highest-ranking eligible candidates to apply for permanent residence. In 2025, invitations were issued to candidates with qualifications in the following categories: French-language proficiency, health care and social services, trades, and education.

In 2025, Canada admitted 114,930 people through EE, including principal applicants and their accompanying spouses and dependants. Of those admitted, 55,810 were female, 59,115 were male, and five were another gender.

For more information on EE, including data across all stages of the EE continuum, please refer to the Express Entry Year End Report.

Federal business programs

Federal Business Programs help attract talented entrepreneurs who can support Canada’s economic growth. These programs include the Start-Up Visa (SUV) Program, which helps entrepreneurs build innovative businesses that can grow and create jobs in Canada. The Self-Employed Persons Program supports individuals with relevant self-employment experience. Application intake is currently paused for both programs.

In 2025, Canada admitted 2,210 principal applicants and their accompanying spouses and dependants through these programs. Of those admitted, 1,090 were female, and 1,115 were male.

Sectoral immigration programs

Sectoral immigration pilot programs test innovative approaches to support specific economic objectives and labour market needs across Canada.

Caregiver programs support seniors, children, and people living with disabilities across Canada by providing home care workers (caregivers) with a pathway to permanent residence. In March 2025, the Home Care Worker Immigration Pilots (HCWIP) were launched, one for Home Child Care workers and one for Home Support workers. Application intake for these pilot programs was paused in December 2025, as part of efforts to return immigration levels to a more sustainable pace and provide greater predictability for clients.

The HCWIP replaced the five-year Home Child Care Provider and Home Support Worker pilots, which ended in June 2024. Applications received before the expiry of caregiver programs continue to be processed. In 2025, 5,840 caregivers and their spouses and dependants were admitted through Caregiver programs.Footnote 36 Of those admitted, 3,430 were female, and 2,410 were male.

The five-year Agri-Food Pilot program (AFP) ended on May 14, 2025. Applications received by that date continue to be processed. The AFP tested an industry-specific approach to immigration to help address longstanding labour needs in Canada’s agriculture and agri-food sector. It provided a pathway to permanent residence for experienced, non-seasonal workers in select year-round occupations. In 2025, 570 people were admitted through the AFP, including principal applicants and their dependants. Of those admitted, 255 were female and 315 were male.

Regional immigration programs and pilots

Regional immigration programs allow provinces, territories, and communities to select immigrants with the skills and experience needed to fill local labour shortages and support regional economic growth.

The Provincial Nominee Program (PNP), Atlantic Immigration Program (AIP), and Community Immigration Pilots Rural and Northern Immigration Pilot (RNIP), Rural Community Immigration Pilot (RCIP) and the Francophone Community Immigration Pilot (FCIP) are designed to respond to evolving regional labour market needs, drive population growth outside major urban centres and promote long-term integration and retention. In 2025, admissions through the PNP, AIP, and Community Immigration Pilots totalled 94,355. These programs accounted for 51% of all economic immigration admissions outside Quebec.

In 2025, Canada admitted 85,120 individuals through the PNP and 5,170 through the AIP,Footnote 37 including principal applicants and their accompanying spouses and dependants. Of those admitted through the PNP, 39,750 were female, 45,365 were male, and a small portion were another gender. For the AIP, 2,470 were female, and 2,700 were male.

The RCIP and the FCIP were launched in 2025, building on the success of the RNIP and testing a new approach to regional and Francophone immigration. These pilots give participating communities a lead role in assessing and recommending candidates that best suit their labour market needs, while collaborating with local stakeholders to create a welcoming environment for newcomers and their families. In 2025, across all rural community programs (RNIP, RCIP & FCIP), 4,060 principal applicants and their accompanying spouses and dependants were admitted as permanent residents through these pilots. Of those admitted, 1,920 were female, and 2,140 were male.

Francophone immigration outside Quebec

Francophone immigration supports regional vitality and economic development, particularly in Francophone Minority Communities across the country (outside Quebec). It also assists broader commitments to linguistic diversity and demographic renewal.

In 2025, IRCC advanced implementation of several initiatives announced in 2024. These included the Francophone Minority Communities Student Pilot, the Centre for Innovation on Francophone Immigration , and the expansion of the Welcoming Francophone Communities Initiative. To complement these initiatives, in 2025, the Department launched the Francophone Community Immigration Pilot, which offers permanent residence to French-speaking and/or bilingual skilled workers who want to work and settle in rural remote Francophone Minority Communities (FMCs) across Canada.

In addition, the Centre for Innovation in Francophone Immigration supported 17 innovative projects in 2025, totalling $3.9M. These projects aimed to reduce barriers to Francophone immigration. Funding supported activities such as the promotion of FMCs internationally, the recruitment and selection of French-speaking and bilingual candidates, and research studies.

Admissions targets for french-speaking permanent residents outside quebec

IRCC’s Policy on Francophone Immigration provides long-term strategic direction to support the vitality and economic development of FMCs and requires that French-speaking permanent resident admissions targets be included in the Multi-Year Levels Plan.

In 2025, IRCC surpassed its 8.5% target, for francophone admissions outside Quebec with 29,650 French-speaking permanent residents admitted outside Quebec, representing 8.9% of overall admissions outside the province.

In 2025, four of the top five source countries for French-speaking permanent residents admitted outside Quebec were in sub-Saharan Africa and North Africa. Cameroon was the largest source country, accounting for 40% of all admissions. These newcomers help strengthen Francophone minority communities across Canada and support Canada’s linguistic and cultural diversity.

Sponsored family

Canada remains committed to helping Canadian citizens and permanent residents sponsor eligible family members for permanent residence. Family sponsorship helps reunite families and supports the social, cultural, and economic strength of communities across Canada. Sponsors agree to provide financial support for a set period to help sponsored family members settle and integrate successfully.

In 2025, 96,170 people were admitted through the Family Sponsorship Program. This included spouses, partners, dependent children, parents and grandparents, and other eligible relatives. Of those admitted, 59,040 were female, 37,115 were male, and 10 were another gender.

Compared with 2024, family sponsorship admissions decreased by 9.3%. In 2024, 105,990 people were admitted through the Sponsored Family category.

In 2025, females made up 61.4% of admissions in the Sponsored Family category. This continues a trend seen over the past five years, with females representing most admissions in this category. This pattern is linked to the composition of sponsored spouses and partners, where females account for a greater share of principal applicants in this category and accompanying family members. Sponsored Family admissions declined from 2024 to 2025 for both females and males. However, the decrease was smaller among females (8%) than among males (11.2%).

India remained the largest source country for Sponsored Family admissions in 2025, accounting for 30% of all admissions. Four source countries are located across Asia, reflecting the diverse backgrounds of Canadian sponsors and their families.

Sponsored spouses, partners, dependent children and othersFootnote 40

In 2025, 71,645 individuals were admitted through the sponsored spouses, partners, and children category. Of those admitted, 44,170 were female, 27,465 were male, and 10 were another gender. Admissions in this category decreased by 8.9% compared with 2024. In 2024, 78,655 people were admitted through the sponsored spouses, partners, and children category.

In 2025, females accounted for 61.6% of admissions under the sponsored spouses, partners, and dependent children category. This continues a trend seen over the past five years, with females making up a growing share of admissions. This pattern reflects the makeup of the program, as females are more often the sponsored spouse or partner. Admissions in this category declined for both females and males in 2025 compared with 2024. However, the decrease was smaller among females (7.5%) than among males (11%).

Sponsored parents and grandparents

As of January 1, 2026, IRCC has paused accepting new permanent resident applications made by parents or grandparents of a sponsor, or related sponsorship applications. Applications received as part of the 2025 intake will be accepted into processing in 2026.

In 2025, 24,525 individuals were admitted through the Sponsored Parents and Grandparents category. Of those admitted, 14,875 were female, and 9,650 were male.

Admissions in this category decreased by 10.3% compared with 2024. In 2024, 27,330 people were admitted through the sponsored parents and grandparents category.

In 2025, females accounted for 60.6% of admissions under the sponsored parents and grandparents category. This continues a trend seen since 2023, with females making up the majority of admissions in this category.

Super visa

Although IRCC paused the intake of Parents and Grandparent program applications on January 1, 2026, parents or grandparents may still apply for a super visa. This super visa is a multi-entry temporary resident visa that allows parents or grandparents to reunite with their Canadian citizen, permanent resident, and registered IndianFootnote 46 family members in Canada for five years per entry. Visitors may also apply to extend their stay by up to two additional years while in Canada.

In 2025, the department approved 52,850 super visa applications. Of the applicants, 32,850 were female, and 20,000 were male. The number of approved super visa applications decreased slightly in 2025. In 2024, IRCC approved 53,695 applications, compared with 52,850 in 2025, a decrease of 1.6%.

Humanitarian & compassionate and other

This category includes people who are granted permanent residence on humanitarian and compassionate grounds, through certain public policies, or under the Permit Holder Class. These measures help people facing exceptional circumstances, including those affected by global events or those who need special support while in Canada. These admissions comprise all Humanitarian and Compassionate and Other admissions, along with the Permit Holder Class.

In 2025, a total of 12,755 individuals were admitted under this category. Of those admitted, 6,780 were female, and 5,975 were male. Admissions under this category decreased significantly compared with 2024. In 2024, 19,355 people were admitted through the Humanitarian and Compassionate and Other category, compared with 12,755 in 2025, a decrease of 34.1%.

In 2025, females accounted for 53.2% of admissions under the Humanitarian & Compassionate and Other category.

The top five source countries for admissions under the Humanitarian and Compassionate and Other category in 2025 reflected a range of ongoing humanitarian situations, including human rights concerns, political and economic instability, armed conflict, and other crises.

Ukraine and China were the two largest source countries, accounting for 38.4% of all admissions in this category. Sudan, Colombia and Haiti rounded out the top five source countries, reflecting continued humanitarian needs in those regions. These admission patterns are consistent with the objectives of the public policies and special measures that support people affected by exceptional circumstances.

Refugees and protected persons

In 2025, a total of 59,165 refugees and protected persons were admitted as permanent residents. Of those admitted, 28,705 were female, 30,450 were male, and 10 were another gender. Admissions in this category decreased by 22.8% compared with 2024. In 2024, Canada admitted 76,685 refugees and protected persons as permanent residents.

Resettled refugees

Each year, IRCC facilitates the admission of a targeted number of permanent residents under the resettled refugee category. These include Convention Refugees who are outside their country of origin and cannot return because they have a well-founded fear of persecution. It also includes people who need resettlement because they have been seriously affected by armed conflict, civil war, or widespread human rights violations. Canada’s resettlement program supports a range of vulnerable groups, including religious and ethnic minorities, LGBTQI+ individuals, human rights defenders, as well as women and children in precarious situations.

In 2025, 38,785 resettled refugees were admitted to Canada as permanent residents. Of these, 18,530 were female, 20,255 were male and five were another gender. Admissions in this category decreased by 21.3% compared with 2024. In 2024, Canada admitted 49,285 resettled refugees as permanent residents.

The Private Sponsorship of Refugees Program accounted for most resettled refugee admissions in 2025. A total of 22,975 privately sponsored refugees were admitted, representing 59.2% of all resettled refugees. Of these, 10,475 were female and 12,500 were male. Females accounted for 45.6% of admissions through the Private Sponsorship of Refugees Program, slightly lower than the 47.8% share of females among all resettled refugees.

The Government-Assisted Refugees (GAR) Program accounted for 40.6% of resettled refugee admissions. In 2025, 15,730 government-assisted refugees were admitted. Of those admitted, 8,010 were female, 7,715 were male, and five were another gender.

The remaining admissions were through the Blended Visa Office-Referred (BVOR) Program. In 2025, 80 BVOR refugees were admitted, 45 were female, and 35 were male.

Protected persons in-Canada

Protected Persons in-Canada (PPIC) can apply for permanent residence and may include their dependants, whether in Canada or abroad. This category includes individuals recognized by the Immigration and Refugee Board of Canada (IRB) as Convention Refugees or persons in need of protection, most individuals with positive pre-removal risk assessments, and protected temporary residents.

In 2025, a total of 20,380 protected persons obtained permanent residence. Of those who obtained permanent residence 10,180 were female, 10,195 were male, and five were another gender. The number of protected persons who obtained permanent residence decreased by 25.6% compared with 2024. In 2024, 27,400 protected persons became permanent residents.

In 2025, Eritrea was the largest source country for resettled refugees admitted to Canada, accounting for 26.5% of all resettled refugee admissions. Afghanistan and Somalia were the next largest source countries.

For Protected Persons in Canada (PPIC), Iran was the largest source country in 2025, accounting for 8% of permanent residence admissions. India and Nigeria were the next largest source countries. These admission patterns reflect the different populations served through Canada’s refugee resettlement and in-country protection programs.

Asylum claimants

The in-Canada asylum system provides a process for determining whether individuals are in need of protection, consistent with Canada’s domestic and international legal obligations.

In 2025, Canada received claims from 113,105 asylum claimants at ports of entry and inland offices across the country. This was a decrease of 34.2% compared with 2024, when 171,840 asylum claimants made claims.

In 2025, Haiti and India were the two largest source countries of asylum claimants in Canada. Together, they accounted for 30.8% of all claimants.

Settlement program

IRCC’s Settlement Program supports newcomers in overcoming barriers to economic, social, and cultural integration into Canadian society. As announced in Budget 2025, IRCC will limit settlement service eligibility for economic immigrants beginning April 1, 2026. The change is intended to encourage newcomers to use services earlier, when they are most helpful, and to ensure support remains available for those who need it most.

Before this change, economic immigrants could access federally funded settlement services from the time they became permanent residents until they became Canadian citizens. Under the new rules, economic class permanent residents will be able to access these services:

  • up to six years after obtaining permanent residence, as of April 1, 2026
  • up to five years after obtaining permanent residence, as of April 1, 2027

These changes apply to current and future economic class permanent residents, including principal applicants and their accompanying spouses and dependants.

In 2025, 501,365 unique clients received settlement services. Of these clients, 492,115 received services in Canada,Footnote 52 15,770 received pre-arrival services,Footnote 53 and 30,760 received support through the Resettlement Assistance Program.Footnote 54

Among all clients who received settlement services, 279,440 were female, 217,515 were male, 45 were another gender, and 4,360 had an unspecified gender. During the same period, 22,595 French-speaking newcomers received settlement services from Francophone service provider organizations.

Gender-based analysis on the impact of the Act

While economic program admissions are broadly gender-balanced, they tend to reflect traditional gender patterns in certain occupational sectors. Within regional economic programs (e.g., Atlantic Immigration, Provincial Nominee, etc.) 91.7% of trade and transport applicants were male, while 73% of healthcare applicants were female. Within federal economic programs, the caregiver pilots have largely female admissions, while most temporary workers in agriculture are male.

Gaps in labour market outcomes for immigrant women have been documented since the 1990s. These gaps are exacerbated when race or ethnicity are also factored. Among full-time workers, the gender wage gaps faced by immigrant women who landed in Canada as adults were about twice as large (19.8%) as those faced by Canadian-born women (8.1%) and immigrant women who landed as children (9.8%). In addition to wage gaps, data from the 2021 census shows that racialized immigrant women aged 25 to 54 had the lowest labour force participation and employment rates, and the highest unemployment rates.

GBA Plus in immigration research

GBA Plus is an important tool for migration policy development. Research shows that immigrant and refugee outcomes are influenced by many factors that can overlap and interact. These factors include gender, race, ethnicity, income, language, disability, and age.

During the reporting period, the department strengthened its GBA Plus capacity through ongoing research that applied an intersectional approach. This work expanded the evidence on how factors such as gender, immigration class, age (including seniors), country of origin, official language status, and disability, affect social and economic outcomes for immigrants in Canada.

A notable area of progress was the integration of disability into immigration research. The December 2025 study Educational attainment and employment among immigrants with disabilities represents a significant advancement in departmental capacity to conduct GBA Plus involving immigrants with disabilities. This work generated new insights on barriers and differential outcomes in education and labour market participation, supporting more inclusive and evidence-based policy analysis and development.

Beyond disability, multiple studies incorporated GBA Plus by examining diverse populations and various intersecting identity characteristics, including:

  • Differences in outcomes across immigration programs (e.g., Economic outcomes of provincial nominees: Differences between Express Entry and non-Express Entry, February 2026);
  • Labour market integration across occupations and sectors, including regulated professions (Immigrant nurses in Canada, September 2025);
  • Outcomes among official language minority communities and Francophone populations (Economic outcomes of official language minority permanent residents, August 2025; Experiences of French-speaking immigrants in rural communities, July 2025);
  • The role of education, including international student program (Fields of study and occupations of immigrants who were international students, July 2025);
  • Socio-economic disparities, including wealth gaps (Trends in the wealth gap between immigrant and Canadian-born families, March 2026);
  • Housing outcomes across immigrant and non-permanent resident groups (Housing use of immigrants and non-permanent residents, May 2025; Immigration and housing prices across municipalities, June 2025).

Collectively, these initiatives strengthen IRCC’s analytical capacity by embedding GBA Plus considerations into core research products, improving disaggregated data use, and supporting a more systematic approach to intersectional analysis across departmental work.

Annex: Canada’s Next Temporary and Permanent Immigration Levels Plan

Under the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act (IRPA), the Minister of Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship must table Canada’s Immigration Levels Plan (Levels Plan) in Parliament each year. Since 2017, a rolling three-year Levels Plan has been presented, which projects permanent resident (PR) admissions to Canada. Beginning in 2024, IRCC expanded the Levels Plan to include targets for new temporary resident (TR) arrivals. This approach supports a well-managed migration system by looking at the totality of newcomers arriving in Canada each year and ensures immigration continues to support our economy and domestic workforce while accounting for impacts on infrastructure, housing and services.

With a commitment to build a whole-of-society approach to immigration planning, IRCC develops the Levels Plan carrying out extensive consultations and engagement with federal partners, provinces and territories, municipalities, stakeholder organizations, and the public.

Temporary resident (TR) targets in the Levels Plan include new arrivals for international students, as well as temporary workers under both the Temporary Foreign Worker Program (TFWP) – jointly delivered with Employment and Social Development Canada (ESDC) – and the International Mobility Program (IMP), administered solely by IRCC.

Permanent residents (PRs) are categorized under three overarching groups: Economic immigration; Family reunification; and, Refugees, Protected Persons, Humanitarian & Compassionate, and Other.

The 2027-2029 Immigration Levels Plan

The Government is continuing to advance the commitment to sustainable immigration by building on progress already made to restore stability and predictability to the system. The Levels Plan aligns with the Government’s broader economic priority to attract the talent and skills needed to complement Canada’s domestic workforce and support major investments, while collaborating with provinces and territories, supporting regional and rural communities and reinforcing Canadians’ confidence in a well-managed immigration system.

Canada has already taken action to reduce immigration volumes and decrease pressures on our infrastructure, housing and services. Achieving sustainable immigration requires stable, predictable targets that align volumes of both temporary and permanent residents to the Canadian context, taking into account the capacity to welcome newcomers and objective to grow our economy. To this end, targets aim to balance supporting Canada’s ambitious economic agenda and meeting labour market needs while prioritizing first and foremost the Canadian domestic workforce. The Plan also demonstrates that Canada is a welcoming country and that our immigration system reflects Canada’s commitment to economic success and compassion as a nation while restoring trust and bringing greater alignment between immigration levels and the capacity of domestic infrastructure and services.

Temporary Residents (TRs)

To balance the Government’s commitment to sustainable immigration with the need for stability, predictability and economic growth, the 2027-2029 Levels Plan stabilizes targets for new temporary arrivals at 370,000 for the next three years. These figures represent new temporary worker and student arrivals. Over the course of the Plan, workers are expected to represent approximately 60% of new arrivals, and students approximately 40%. Workers are prioritized to help meet critical labour market needs to complement Canada’s domestic workforce.

Temporary resident targets provide stability and predictability for provinces and territories, employers, and educational institutions while supporting short-term labour needs tied to major investments and helping Canada attract global talent while continuing to work towards the Government’s commitment to reduce the non-permanent resident population. Targets are allocated under the Temporary Foreign Worker Program and the International Mobility Program.

Permanent Residents (PRs)

Permanent resident admissions will remain at 380,000 for all three years of the 2027-2029 Levels Plan. As supporting the Canadian economy continues to be a key priority, the economic category remains the largest proportion of admissions each year at 64.4% across all three years. It supports a stronger focus on selecting the talent Canada needs to drive growth, support innovation, fill persistent labour shortages, and strengthen rural and remote communities while modestly increasing provincial programs to address regional labour needs.

Targets for the Family, Refugees and Protected Persons, and Humanitarian & Compassionate and Other categories remain stable, with the proportions of overall admissions across all three years remaining at 21.3% for Family, 13% for Refugees and Protected Persons, and 1.3% for Humanitarian & Compassionate and Other.

The Plan reinforces Canada’s commitment to ensuring the vitality and economic development of Francophone Minority Communities, by setting targets for French-speaking permanent resident admissions outside Quebec at 9.5% in 2027, 10.5% in 2028, and 12% in 2029.

Time-Limited Measure

IRCC is introducing a new time-limited measure to provide additional space to process permanent residence applications in 2027 within two cohorts:

  • First, the workers cohort will transition in-Canada workers and their accompanying family members to permanent residence in collaboration with provinces and territories in order to support regional labour market needs.
  • Second, the humanitarian cohort will transition individuals to permanent residence who are already in Canada and have been waiting for many years. This transition will provide fairness, keep families together, and fulfill some of Canada’s existing humanitarian commitments.

This time-limited measure complements the broader objectives of the 2027–2029 Levels Plan, and ensures that immigration will continue to serve its core purposes: supporting economic growth while prioritizing the domestic workforce, maintaining the integrity and security of the system, reuniting families, and providing stability in Canada for the humanitarian cohorts we have committed to support.
